The problem

It is not the sending. It is the remembering.

Who has not sent it yet. Whether the file that arrived is the one you asked for. Whether the deadline is close enough to ring them. And then next year, the same forty documents, typed out again from nothing.

What the tools for this cost you

The closest product to this one charges $35 a month for 20 open requests, $71 for 50 and $119 for 100 — and caps how many people you can have on every tier. A practice with sixty clients mid-season is pushed onto the $119 plan, about £92. That is per-client pricing wearing a different hat.

And the larger ones will not sell to you

The enterprise tools in this space gate everything behind a demo. The form asks how many professionals you have. Nobody at a three-person practice is going to take that call, which means those tools are not really an option — they are a category you read about.

“I have asked 20 million times — give me your last tax documents that was filed.”
A bookkeeper, describing the problem in a practitioner group, September 2026

What it does

The whole job, and nothing else

One page for your practice, one link per client. No modules you will never open, no features that exist to fill a pricing page.

A checklist per client

Bank statements, ID, the signed engagement letter — one item each, so a utility bill cannot satisfy the letter. Save the list as a template and use it again next year.

A link they open with no account

No app, no password, no sign-up. Your client opens it, sees who is asking and what is outstanding, and sends. One unguessable address, with an expiry you choose.

Chase without being a nuisance

See who is outstanding, and the tool writes the reminder — including the reason, if you rejected something. You set the cadence, and a phone call you log counts as being in touch, so nobody gets rung and emailed on the same morning.

Know when it is actually finished

A request stops being outstanding when every document has arrived and been checked, so “ready to work on” means ready. And you are told when a client does their part, so you are not the last to know about your own work.

A record of what was asked

Every ask, every file, every call, every rejection, with dates. So “I asked you on the 3rd and then again on the 10th” is something you can read rather than remember — and something you can show.

The client sees their own receipt

Their page says how many documents they have sent and names each file back to them, with the date. So “did you get it?” is a question they can answer themselves rather than phoning to ask.

Your client’s end

One link, no account, and no excuses

This is the whole of what your client has to deal with. They open the link, see your name at the top so they know who is asking, and work down the list. No app to install, no password to forget, and nothing to sign up for.

It names the files back to them as they arrive, so “did you get it?” is a question they can answer without ringing you — and if something you sent was not what was needed, your reason sits beside the item rather than in an email they have lost.

Nothing here needs an account

The link is an unguessable address with an expiry you choose, not a login. It works once you send it and stops working when you say so — and you can issue a new one at any time without disturbing the files already there.

They can tell you they cannot

Beside every item there are two buttons: I do not have this and I will send it later. A client who is stuck says so instead of going quiet, which is the difference between a request you can act on and one you keep chasing.

And the file never leaves their machine in the clear

Their browser encrypts each document to your key before it is sent, so it is already sealed by the time it reaches any server — including ours. They are not being asked to trust a portal; they are handing you an envelope.

The difference

Other tools read your clients’ documents. This one cannot.

The leading product in this space advertises “AI agents that pre-screen documents as they arrive” — which cannot work unless the platform can open every file, in full, on its own servers. That is a real feature. It is also the opposite of this one, and choosing between them is a choice about where your clients’ bank statements live.

What that buys you, concretely.

Nobody at this end can read a client document — not support, not an engineer, not whoever runs the server. There is no back door to forget to close, because there is no key here to close one with.

And the honest limit, which we would rather you read here.

If you lose your passphrase, nobody can recover the documents — us included. That is what “we cannot read them” means. The tool makes you save it somewhere safe before you can send your first link, and you can add a colleague who holds their own copy. But it is a real consequence, not a footnote.

If those last two lines are fine with you, this is the better option and it always will be.

Self-hosted

Three commands, then it is yours

One container, one volume. No external database, no third-party service, no account to create, and no dependencies to install — the runtime and the database are the two that are already inside Node. 

# needs Docker and nothing else
git clone https://github.com/Caseymccallum/tickmark
cd tickmark
docker compose up -d

Then open http://localhost:3000

Client links need HTTPS in anything but a local trial, so the documented path puts a reverse proxy in front of it — one page in the README, rather than a certificate story invented here.

Why we can say the install works

Because a script checks it rather than our word: the container is built, run, and driven through the whole cycle — uploads, a key rotation, the re-encryption pass that follows it — then restarted to confirm the volume kept the records. It is in the repository, so you can point it at your own container.

Questions

The ones people actually ask

Including the two that are awkward. If something here is wrong or missing, that is worth knowing about — the source is public, and the design document lists what this deliberately does not do.

If you cannot read the files, how do you make any money?

By running the server for practices that would rather not. That is the entire hosted product: the same software, on a machine you do not have to look after. Nothing is sold back to you that we could read, because we cannot read it.

What happens if I stop paying?

Your practice is locked out of the hosted service until it is settled — and your clients’ links keep working while that is sorted out, because somebody mid-collection should not be stranded by their accountant’s card.

You can export everything and move to your own server at any time. The software does not disappear, because it was never ours to take away.

Can I move between hosted and self-hosted?

Yes. Moving in is one command in the repository, and moving out is a copy of the data directory. It is a single SQLite file plus the encrypted uploads beside it, which is the whole reason both directions are easy.

Is there a free plan?

Running it yourself is the free plan, and it is not a cut-down version — it is the same software with the same limits, which is to say none.

There is no free tier of the hosted service. A free tier is paid for by somebody, and we would rather charge the people who use it than the people who do not.

What about GDPR, and where the data actually lives?

Whichever way you run it, documents are encrypted before they reach any server and the key stays with your practice — so what a host holds is ciphertext.

On your own server, nothing leaves hardware you control and there is no processor to name in your own records. On ours, you get a data processing agreement and the honest statement that we hold files we cannot open.

Does it integrate with my tax software?

No. Lacerte, ProSeries, Drake, UltraTax and the rest are a different product with a different support burden, and building toward them would mean slowly becoming the thing this exists as an alternative to.

What comes out is a CSV of the season — every client, every document, every date — which is what most practices actually want when they say integration.

Is it really open source?

Yes — AGPL-3.0, which is an OSI-approved open-source licence. The repository is public, you can read every line that matters — the encryption code, the checks that keep a server from reading a document, the tests — and you can run the whole thing yourself for nothing.

The one thing the licence asks of anybody offering it to others as a service is that they publish the changes they made. That is deliberate: it means a competitor can host this, but not as a black box, and improvements come back rather than disappearing into a closed fork.

What it does not do is stop somebody hosting this and charging for it — and that is the honest position. The hosted edition above competes by being run well: the servers, the support, the backups, and somebody to ring. If that turns out not to be worth £49 to somebody, they should run it themselves, and the licence is written so that they can.

Nothing on this page asks you to take on trust anything you cannot check in five minutes. That is not a marketing position; it is the only way any of the claims above are worth reading.
